# Episode 2 — Tailor Your Resume for Every Job

**Topic:** Customizing a resume to match a specific job description.
**Runtime:** ~8–10 minutes.

[Scene: The Career Coach and Job Seeker sit at a desk with a job description open on a laptop between them.]

Coach: Last time we made your bullets stronger. Today we're going to make them relevant, because the same resume doesn't work for every job.

Job Seeker: That's what I've been doing — sending the same version to everyone. Is that really a problem?

[Scene: Close up on the laptop screen showing a job posting with several keywords highlighted.]

Coach: It's the difference between getting noticed and getting skipped. Recruiters and screening software both scan for the specific words in the job posting. If your resume doesn't mirror them, you might not pass the first filter.

Job Seeker: So it's about keywords? I've heard of applicant tracking systems. Do I need to game them?

[Scene: The Coach shakes their head gently, then points at the screen.]

Coach: Not game them. Match them honestly. If the posting asks for "customer onboarding," and your resume says "client setup," you're describing the same skill with different words. Use their words when they're accurate.

Job Seeker: Can you walk me through how you'd actually do that step by step?

[Scene: Screen share of a job description. The Coach scrolls and highlights phrases one at a time.]

Coach: First, read the posting and highlight the nouns and verbs that repeat. Things like "SQL," "cross-functional," or "quarterly planning." Those are your target terms. Then find where your real experience lines up with each one.

Job Seeker: What if I don't have a match for every requirement?

[Scene: Close up on the Job Seeker looking uncertain.]

Coach: That's normal. Most people meet sixty to seventy percent of a posting. If you meet the core requirements — the ones listed first or mentioned twice — you're in a reasonable range to apply.

Job Seeker: Should I reorder my bullets for each application?

[Scene: The Coach rearranges index cards on the desk, moving one card to the top.]

Coach: Yes. Put your most relevant bullet first under each role. Recruiters skim, so the first line of your most recent job carries the most weight. Lead with the experience that matches this posting.

Job Seeker: What about the skills section? Should that change too?

[Scene: Screen share of a skills section being edited, with matching terms moving to the front.]

Coach: Definitely. Reorder so the skills the job asks for appear first, and remove ones that aren't relevant. A focused list reads as intentional rather than generic.

Job Seeker: Do I need to rewrite my summary for every single job?

[Scene: The Coach leans back and taps the screen thoughtfully.]

Coach: At minimum, tweak the last line of your summary to name the exact role and company type you're targeting. It's a small edit that makes the whole document feel tailored.

Job Seeker: That feels manageable. But what if a job posting is vague or full of fluff?

[Scene: The Coach scrolls through a vague posting, frowning slightly.]

Coach: Look for the concrete skills buried in it. If it's genuinely vague, you can't tailor to it — so focus on your strongest, clearest accomplishments instead. You can't match what isn't stated.

Job Seeker: Is there a risk of tailoring too much, like copying the posting word for word?

[Scene: Close up on the Coach's hands making a "stop" gesture.]

Coach: Yes, don't copy whole phrases. Hiring managers can tell when someone pasted their own text back at them. Mirror individual skills, but keep your own voice in the bullets.

Job Seeker: How do I keep track of multiple tailored versions without losing my mind?

[Scene: Screen share of a folder with clearly named files like "resume_product_manager.pdf" and "resume_data_analyst.pdf".]

Coach: Save a master resume with everything, then save a separate file for each application named after the role. That way your strongest version is always one small edit away.

Job Seeker: What about the jobs I've applied to in the past with the generic resume? Did I waste those?

[Scene: The Coach smiles reassuringly.]

Coach: Not at all. Every application is practice, and now you have a better method going forward. You can't undo the past, but you can improve every application from here on.

Job Seeker: That's good to hear. Can we look at one of my actual target postings together?

[Scene: The Coach pulls up a posting for a marketing coordinator role.]

Coach: Let's do it. I see "email campaigns," "analytics," and "collaboration" repeated. Do you have experience with any of those?

Job Seeker: I ran a small email newsletter and reviewed open rates. I didn't think of it as "campaigns."

[Scene: Close up on the Job Seeker brightening as they realize the connection.]

Coach: There it is. That's your "email campaigns" bullet, and it's more relevant to this role than half the generic bullets on your current resume.

Job Seeker: This is starting to click. Tailoring isn't about lying, it's about surfacing the right examples.

[Scene: The Coach nods and closes the laptop.]

Coach: Exactly. Next time we'll write a cover letter that reinforces the same story. For now, pick one posting and tailor one version of your resume to it.

Job Seeker: I'll do that tonight. One question first — how long should tailoring take?

[Scene: Wide shot of the Coach standing to shake hands.]

Coach: For one focused application, about twenty to thirty minutes. The more you practice, the faster it gets. A strong match is worth the time.

[Scene: The Job Seeker sits back down, a new thought forming.]

Job Seeker: One more thing — what if I have years of experience that's completely unrelated to the job I want now?

[Scene: Close up on the Coach nodding thoughtfully.]

Coach: Then mine it for transferable skills. "Unrelated" rarely means useless. Managing a team, handling budgets, and solving customer problems all travel across industries. Lead with those instead of the niche details.

Job Seeker: So even a retail job has skills for an office role?

[Scene: The Coach spreads their hands to show a wide range.]

Coach: Absolutely. Scheduling, conflict resolution, cash handling, and training new staff are all real, transferable skills. It's about translating them into the language of the job you want.

Job Seeker: Should I change my job title to match the posting, even if my official title was different?

[Scene: The Coach shakes their head and points at the resume.]

Coach: Keep your official title, but you can add a short parenthetical if it clarifies. For example, "Customer Associate (Account Management)." That's honest and helps the reader understand your actual function.

Job Seeker: What about the experience that isn't relevant — do I cut it entirely?

[Scene: Screen share of a resume where an old, irrelevant role shrinks to a single line.]

Coach: You don't have to delete it, but you can shrink it. Give unrelated roles one or two lines instead of a full block. That keeps your history intact while putting the spotlight on what matters.

Job Seeker: How do I know if a posting is even worth tailoring for, versus a stretch where I'm wasting time?

[Scene: The Coach tilts their head, weighing the question.]

Coach: If you meet most of the must-have requirements, tailor and apply. If you're missing several core ones, it's probably a stretch. Spend your time where the fit is real — you'll get more interviews per hour that way.

Job Seeker: That's a practical filter. And once I tailor a resume, should I tweak the cover letter too?

[Scene: The Coach nods and opens both documents side by side.]

Coach: Yes, always keep them in sync. If your resume now leads with email campaigns, your cover letter should mention the same example. Consistency across documents makes you look intentional.

Job Seeker: This all ties together so much more than I expected.

[Scene: Wide shot of the Coach standing to shake hands again, smiling.]

Coach: It's all one story, told a few different ways. Tailor it honestly, keep it consistent, and you'll give yourself a far better shot than a generic resume ever could.
