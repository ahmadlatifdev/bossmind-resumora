# Script 1 — Resume-to-Interview Mastery (ATS Optimization)

**Duration:** 8 minutes | **Word count:** ~1,200 | **Language:** English

---

## [0:00–0:30] HOOK

Seventy-five percent of qualified candidates are rejected before a human ever sees their resume. Not because they lack skills. Not because they lack experience. Because an algorithm — the Applicant Tracking System — decided they were not a match. In the next eight minutes, I will show you exactly how to beat that algorithm, and then how to turn your resume into interview talking points that win the room.

## [0:30–2:00] SEGMENT 1 — Understanding the ATS

An ATS is not a judge. It is a filter. It scans your resume for specific keywords, checks your formatting, and ranks you against the job description. If your resume uses a two-column layout, graphics, or unusual fonts, the system may fail to read it entirely. If your resume says "managed a team" but the job description says "led cross-functional teams," the algorithm sees a mismatch — even though you are fully qualified.

Here is what the ATS actually reads: plain text. That is it. It strips away your elegant design and reads what remains. So your first job is simple — make sure what remains is exactly what the algorithm is looking for.

Key point: Format for the machine first. Then format for the human.

## [2:00–4:00] SEGMENT 2 — Aligning Keywords With the Job Description

Take the job description. Copy it into a document. Highlight every noun and every verb that repeats. Those are the keywords. "Stakeholder management." "Data-driven decision making." "Cross-functional collaboration." "Python." "Salesforce." These are not decoration — they are the vocabulary the ATS is scoring against.

Now look at your resume. Does it use that exact vocabulary? Not synonyms. Not paraphrases. The exact words. If the job says "stakeholder management," your resume should say "stakeholder management" — not "worked with clients."

Practice exercise: Pause this video. Take the last job you applied for. Copy its description. Highlight every repeated term. Then open your resume and count how many of those terms appear verbatim. If the number is below ten, you have work to do.

## [4:00–6:00] SEGMENT 3 — Turning ATS Gains Into Interview Talking Points

Here is the part most candidates miss. The keywords you add to beat the ATS become your interview script. Every keyword is a story waiting to be told.

If your resume says "led a cross-functional team of twelve to deliver a product launch two weeks ahead of schedule," that is not just a resume line. That is a STAR story. Situation: the launch was at risk. Task: you had to coordinate twelve people across three departments. Action: you restructured the timeline and negotiated dependencies. Result: delivered two weeks early.

Prepare three to five of these stories. Each one anchored to a keyword in your resume. When the interviewer asks about teamwork, you pull out the cross-functional story. When they ask about deadlines, you pull the same story from a different angle. You are not improvising. You are delivering the exact narrative you already proved on paper.

## [6:00–7:30] SEGMENT 4 — The Project-First Format

Whether you are a fresher or a senior professional, the most effective resume format in 2026 is project-first. Lead with what you built, not where you worked.

For each project, write three lines: the problem, your action, the measurable result. "Reduced customer churn by eighteen percent by rebuilding the onboarding flow." "Cut report generation time from six hours to twenty minutes by automating the data pipeline." These lines survive ATS parsing because they are plain text. And they survive human scrutiny because they are specific.

Senior profiles should also include a one-line impact summary at the top: "Product leader with twelve years delivering B2B SaaS products that generated over forty million dollars in ARR." This line is what the human reads first. It is also what the ATS extracts as your headline.

## [7:30–8:00] CLOSING RECAP

Three things. One — format for the ATS first: plain text, no graphics, single column. Two — mirror the job description vocabulary verbatim; not synonyms. Three — turn every keyword into a STAR story you can deliver in the interview.

Do this, and you are no longer in the seventy-five percent. You are the candidate who gets the callback. In the next video, I will show you exactly how to structure your STAR answers so they land with maximum impact. See you there.
