# Episode 1 — Build a Strong Resume

**Topic:** Writing achievement-driven resume bullets that show impact.
**Runtime:** ~8–10 minutes.

[Scene: Wide shot of a warm, modern coaching office. The Career Coach sits across from the Job Seeker, both holding a printed resume.]

Coach: Let's start with the single most common mistake I see on resumes: people describe their daily duties instead of their results.

Job Seeker: That's exactly what I did. I listed things like "managed the team calendar" and "answered customer emails."

[Scene: Close up on the Coach nodding, then pointing at a line on the resume.]

Coach: And those are tasks, not outcomes. A recruiter reading that can't tell whether you did those things well. We're going to turn every duty into a result.

Job Seeker: How do I do that if my job didn't have a number attached to it?

[Scene: Screen share of a resume. A highlighted bullet reads "Managed team calendar." A cursor draws a red circle around it.]

Coach: Great question, and it's the reason most people get stuck. Not every result is a revenue figure. You can also show scale, speed, quality, or scope. Think of four questions: how many, how fast, how much better, and for how many people.

Job Seeker: Can you give me a concrete example from my own resume?

[Scene: Close up of the Coach writing on a whiteboard. The words "Action + Result" appear in large letters.]

Coach: Sure. You wrote "answered customer emails." Let's ask the four questions. If you handled forty emails a day and cut average response time by a third, that's your bullet.

Job Seeker: So "answered customer emails" becomes "resolved forty customer emails daily, reducing average response time by thirty percent."

[Scene: Screen share showing the before-and-after bullets side by side, the new one glowing gold.]

Coach: Exactly. Now a hiring manager sees proof, not a to-do list. Start every bullet with a strong verb, and end it with the outcome whenever you can.

Job Seeker: What counts as a strong verb?

[Scene: The Coach turns a laptop toward the Job Seeker, revealing a list of action verbs.]

Coach: Words like "led," "built," "reduced," "delivered," "launched," and "resolved." Avoid weak openers like "responsible for" or "helped with." They bury your contribution.

Job Seeker: I was part of a team for most projects. Is it dishonest to say "led" if I didn't run the whole thing?

[Scene: Close up on the Job Seeker looking slightly concerned, then the Coach leaning in reassuringly.]

Coach: That's a fair worry. You shouldn't claim work you didn't do. Instead, be specific about your slice. "Led the migration of the customer database" is honest if you ran that piece.

Job Seeker: That feels much more truthful. So it's accuracy plus emphasis, not exaggeration.

[Scene: The Coach smiles and taps the table twice.]

Coach: Precisely. Employers want the truth, but they also want to understand the scope of what you actually accomplished.

Job Seeker: How long should each bullet be? I've seen resumes with huge paragraphs.

[Scene: Screen share of a resume where one dense paragraph is highlighted, then it shrinks into three short bullets.]

Coach: Keep most bullets to one or two lines. If a bullet needs three lines, you're probably hiding two separate accomplishments. Split them.

Job Seeker: And how many bullets per job?

[Scene: Close up on the resume, fingers pointing to the bullets under each role.]

Coach: Four to six bullets for your most recent or most relevant role, and fewer for older positions. Quality beats quantity every time.

Job Seeker: What if my result isn't a number I can prove? Like "improved team morale."

[Scene: The Coach tilts their head, thinking, then leans forward.]

Coach: Then describe the visible evidence of that change. "Launched a recognition program adopted by thirty teammates" is concrete even without a percentage.

Job Seeker: So I can quantify people, programs, or processes, not just money.

[Scene: Close up on the Coach nodding firmly.]

Coach: Yes. Money is the easiest to measure, but headcount, turnaround time, and adoption rates all work just as well.

Job Seeker: What about the summary at the top? Do I even need one?

[Scene: Wide shot of the Coach leaning back, then pointing to the top of the page.]

Coach: A short summary helps if it's specific. Two or three sentences naming your role, experience level, and the kind of work you want next. Skip buzzwords like "passionate" and "hardworking."

Job Seeker: Because everyone writes those, right?

[Scene: The Coach chuckles and nods.]

Coach: Exactly, and they don't distinguish you. "Product manager with six years in B2B SaaS" says far more than "passionate professional."

Job Seeker: What about the skills section? Should I list every tool I've ever touched?

[Scene: Screen share of a skills section overflowing with keywords, then it trims down to a clean list.]

Coach: No. List the skills that are relevant to the role you want, and be honest about your level. Listing a tool you used once can backfire in an interview.

Job Seeker: And education? My degree is ten years old now.

[Scene: The Coach gestures to the bottom of the page.]

Coach: Keep it simple. Degree, school, and year. Once you have work experience, education moves to the bottom and doesn't need coursework or GPA.

Job Seeker: This is already changing how I think about my resume. What should I tackle first tonight?

[Scene: The Coach hands the Job Seeker a single sheet of paper with three steps printed on it.]

Coach: Three things. One, rewrite your most recent role into achievement bullets using Action plus Result. Two, swap every weak verb for a strong one. Three, trim anything longer than two lines.

Job Seeker: I can do that. But what if I'm still not sure my result is impressive enough to include?

[Scene: Close up on the Job Seeker, then the Coach smiling warmly.]

Coach: If it moved the work forward, it matters. And remember — we're improving how you're seen, not promising an outcome. A strong, honest resume gives you a better chance to be considered.

Job Seeker: That's a relief. No magic guarantees, just a clearer story.

[Scene: The Job Seeker pauses, then asks a follow-up with a furrowed brow.]

Job Seeker: What if I genuinely don't know the exact number for something? Is it better to leave it out?

[Scene: The Coach holds up a hand, palm open, to signal reassurance.]

Coach: You can use an honest range instead of a precise figure. "Roughly twenty percent" or "over thirty accounts" is fine when you're estimating from memory. Just never invent a number you can't defend.

Job Seeker: That's a relief, because I don't have reports for everything. What about the tense I use for my current job versus past jobs?

[Scene: Screen share of a resume where "manage" is highlighted in the current role and "managed" in an older one.]

Coach: Use present tense for your current role and past tense for everything you've left. Mixing them is a small error, but it makes the resume look less polished.

Job Seeker: I've also been adding a few images and a fancy header. Is that okay?

[Scene: The Coach shakes their head and points at a clean, single-column template.]

Coach: I'd avoid it. Images, tables, and multiple columns can confuse applicant tracking software. Stick to a single column, standard fonts, and simple section headings. Let the content stand out, not the decoration.

Job Seeker: So the resume should be boring on the outside and sharp on the inside?

[Scene: The Coach laughs and nods.]

Coach: That's a great way to put it. Clean on the outside, compelling on the inside. Recruiters scan in seconds, so clarity is worth more than creativity here.

Job Seeker: How long should the whole resume be? I've heard one page, but I have ten years of experience.

[Scene: The Coach gestures to show two pages stacked.]

Coach: One page if you have less than ten years of experience, two pages if you have more. The key is that every line earns its place. Don't pad to hit two pages.

Job Seeker: And if I've had multiple roles at the same company — promotions — how do I show that?

[Scene: Screen share of a resume where one company has two stacked role titles.]

Coach: List the company once, then stack the roles underneath with their own bullets and dates. It shows growth without making you look like you job-hopped.

Job Seeker: I think I've got it. Anything I should do before I consider it done?

[Scene: The Coach leans back and counts on their fingers.]

Coach: Yes — read it out loud, then have someone else read it too. Typos and awkward phrasing hide best on a screen. A second pair of eyes catches what you've stopped seeing.

Job Seeker: I'll do that. This has completely changed how I'll approach it.

[Scene: The Coach nods and closes the laptop, the office light warm behind them.]

Coach: Right. Next time we'll talk about tailoring that story to a specific job. For now, rewrite one role, and you'll already be ahead of most applicants.
