# Script 4 — Multi-Language & Global Career Positioning

**Duration:** 8 minutes | **Word count:** ~1,200 | **Language:** English

---

## [0:00–0:30] HOOK

The same interview answer lands differently in London, Mumbai, Toronto, and Sydney. Culture shapes what counts as confident versus arrogant, direct versus rude, structured versus robotic. In the next eight minutes, I will show you how to adapt your interview style to the market you are targeting — and how to position yourself as a multi-market candidate.

## [0:30–2:00] SEGMENT 1 — The Four Interview Cultures

United States: Direct. Confident. Results-first. Speak about your impact without hedging. "I led the project and we shipped two weeks early." Modesty is a liability here.

United Kingdom: Measured. Self-deprecating. Understated. "We managed to deliver ahead of schedule — a team effort." Overt self-promotion reads as arrogant.

India: Structured. Respectful. Detail-oriented. Interviewers expect process, not just outcome. Walk through your reasoning step by step.

Canada and Australia: Collaborative. Balanced. Team-first. "I worked with the team to ship two weeks early." Individual credit is secondary.

Key point: Same story. Different emphasis. Know the market before you speak.

## [2:00–4:00] SEGMENT 2 — Body Language Across Borders

Eye contact. In the US, UK, Canada, and Australia — steady and warm. In parts of Asia, sustained direct eye contact can feel confrontational. In a video interview, look at the camera lens, not the screen.

Hands. Visible and calm. Some cultures read large gestures as confidence. Others read them as instability. The safest default is a slow, small gesture that matches your point.

Pace. Speak at the pace of your target market. If you are interviewing in the US, faster and more energetic. In the UK, slightly slower. In India, measured and clear.

## [4:00–6:00] SEGMENT 3 — Market-Tuned AI Job Search

You can now run the same job search across five markets simultaneously. But the application must be localized.

Three steps. One — rewrite your resume headline for each market. "Results-driven product leader" works in the US. "Product leader with a collaborative approach" works in Canada. Two — adjust your LinkedIn location signal to your target market, not your current one. Three — mirror the job description vocabulary in the local language. If the role is in France, the resume must be in French.

Use AI to translate, but never to localize. Localization requires cultural judgment. Translation is mechanical. You must review every word.

## [6:00–7:30] SEGMENT 4 — Positioning for a Global Career

The strongest global candidates share three traits. One — they speak at least two languages fluently, even if one is imperfect. Two — they can describe their work in a way that is understandable to any market. Three — they have worked across at least two cultural contexts, even remotely.

If you are at the start of your global career, do not wait for the perfect opportunity. Apply to remote roles across two or three markets. Even one international project on your resume changes how every future employer reads you.

Positioning statement template: "Product leader with eight years of experience delivering B2B SaaS across India, the UK, and remote-first US teams. Fluent in English and Hindi. Focused on scaling products in regulated markets." This statement works in every interview, in every market, because it is specific and it proves range.

## [7:30–8:00] CLOSING RECAP

Three things. One — the same story lands differently per market; adapt the emphasis, not the substance. Two — body language, pace, and eye contact are cultural choices, not universal rules. Three — position yourself as a multi-market candidate, because the future of work is borderless.

You now have all four interview training modules. Apply them. Rehearse them. And walk into every interview — in any language, in any market — ready to win.
