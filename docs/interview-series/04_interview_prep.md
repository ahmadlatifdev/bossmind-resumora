# Episode 4 — Interview Preparation

**Topic:** Preparing answers, questions, and mindset for a successful interview.
**Runtime:** ~8–10 minutes.

[Scene: The Career Coach sits across from the Job Seeker in the office, a notepad open between them. The mood is focused.]

Coach: We've built your resume and cover letter. Now we prepare for the conversation itself. Interviews aren't about being perfect — they're about being prepared.

Job Seeker: That's exactly what makes me nervous. I never know what they're going to ask, so I feel like I can't prepare enough.

[Scene: Close up on the Coach smiling and leaning forward.]

Coach: That's a common feeling, but here's the good news: most interviews follow a predictable shape. They ask about your background, your motivation, and a few behavior questions. You can prepare for all three.

Job Seeker: Let's start with the one everyone dreads: "Tell me about yourself."

[Scene: The Coach holds up three fingers, counting down as they speak.]

Coach: This is your sixty-second commercial. Cover three things in order: who you are now, the relevant experience you bring, and why you're interested in this role. Don't recite your whole resume.

Job Seeker: Can you show me what that sounds like?

[Scene: Screen share of three short bullet points, each lighting up in turn.]

Coach: Something like: "I'm a customer success manager with five years in B2B software. I've spent the last two years leading a team of four and reducing churn by eighteen percent. I'm excited about this role because it combines that experience with a bigger strategic scope."

Job Seeker: That's tight and clear. What about the behavioral questions like "tell me about a time you failed"?

[Scene: The Coach leans back and nods slowly.]

Coach: For those, use the STAR method. Situation, Task, Action, Result. It keeps your answer structured so you don't ramble. And never skip the Result — that's where the learning shows.

Job Seeker: I always forget the result part. I get caught up explaining the problem.

[Scene: Close up on the Job Seeker nodding with recognition, then the Coach pointing at the notepad.]

Coach: Most people do. So write your stories out ahead of time, and make sure each one ends with what you learned or what changed. A story without a result is just a complaint.

Job Seeker: How many stories should I prepare?

[Scene: The Coach flips through the notepad, revealing a list of story titles.]

Coach: Aim for five or six versatile stories. One about a conflict, one about a failure, one about a success, one about teamwork, and one about leadership. You can adapt them to many questions.

Job Seeker: What if they ask something I genuinely don't know the answer to?

[Scene: The Coach holds up a hand in a calm gesture.]

Coach: Be honest. Say you don't know, then explain how you'd find out. Employers respect honesty far more than a made-up answer. It's better to show your thinking than to bluff.

Job Seeker: What kind of questions should I ask them at the end?

[Scene: Screen share of a short list of thoughtful questions.]

Coach: Ask questions that show you've thought about the role, not ones you could Google. Things like "What does success look like in the first ninety days?" or "What's the biggest challenge the team is facing right now?"

Job Seeker: Is there a question I should never ask?

[Scene: The Coach tilts their head and smiles.]

Coach: Don't lead with salary or vacation time. It's fine to ask later in the process, but opening with it can signal the wrong priority. Focus first on whether the work and team are a fit.

Job Seeker: How should I handle nerves before and during the interview?

[Scene: The Coach gestures toward the window, then back to the Job Seeker.]

Coach: Prepare, then trust that preparation. Practice your answers out loud the day before. And remember that a little nervous energy is normal — even experienced candidates feel it. It's not a sign you're failing.

Job Seeker: What about virtual interviews specifically? I have one next week.

[Scene: Screen share of a video call window with the Job Seeker's face in a well-lit frame.]

Coach: Test your camera and microphone beforehand. Look at the lens when you speak, not the screen, so it feels like eye contact. And choose a quiet, tidy background.

Job Seeker: Should I have notes nearby for a virtual interview?

[Scene: The Coach nods and points to the edge of the screen.]

Coach: Yes, but keep them minimal — a few bullet points, not full scripts. Reading from a script is obvious on camera and breaks your connection with the interviewer.

Job Seeker: What if I freeze up and lose my train of thought?

[Scene: Close up on the Coach speaking reassuringly.]

Coach: Pause and take a breath. You can say "That's a great question, let me think for a moment." A short, calm pause reads as thoughtful, not as weakness.

Job Seeker: How do I follow up after the interview?

[Scene: The Coach opens an email draft on the laptop.]

Coach: Send a short thank-you note within a day. Thank them for their time, reference one specific thing you discussed, and restate your interest briefly. It's a small touch that keeps you memorable.

Job Seeker: What if I don't get the job? It's hard not to take that personally.

[Scene: The Coach leans forward with a kind expression.]

Coach: Rejection is part of the process, not a verdict on you. Each interview is practice. Ask for feedback when you can, and use it to improve the next one. Every conversation makes you better.

Job Seeker: That's a healthier way to see it. So the goal is preparation and consistency, not perfection.

[Scene: The Coach nods and closes the notepad.]

Coach: Exactly. None of this guarantees an offer — no one can promise that. But walking in prepared, calm, and honest gives you your best chance to show who you are. That's the real win.

[Scene: The Job Seeker leans forward, wanting to go deeper.]

Job Seeker: Can we walk through a full STAR example together? I want to hear one end to end.

[Scene: The Coach sits up straighter and speaks as if in an interview.]

Coach: Sure. Situation — "Our team was missing our release deadline." Task — "I was asked to get the launch back on track." Action — "I cut the scope to the essential features and set up daily check-ins." Result — "We shipped on time and kept the core customers we'd promised." Notice how tight each part is.

Job Seeker: That's so much clearer than the way I've been telling stories. How do I keep them from going too long?

[Scene: The Coach holds up a hand to mime a time limit.]

Coach: Aim for about ninety seconds per answer. If you practice with a timer, you'll naturally learn to cut the fluff. The goal is complete, not exhaustive.

Job Seeker: What if the interviewer asks a vague question like "tell me about a challenge you faced"?

[Scene: The Coach shrugs slightly and smiles.]

Coach: Pick your strongest prepared story and adapt it. Vague questions are actually an opportunity — they let you choose the example that shows you best. Have one go-to story ready for that exact prompt.

Job Seeker: How early should I ask about the team or the day-to-day of the role?

[Scene: The Coach gestures toward a notepad with questions.]

Coach: Anytime it's relevant. Good interviews are two-way conversations. Asking thoughtful questions throughout shows engagement, not just at the end when they say "any questions for us?"

Job Seeker: What's one thing people forget that makes a real difference?

[Scene: The Coach leans in, lowering their voice for emphasis.]

Coach: Sending a thank-you note. It sounds small, but it's surprisingly rare. A short, specific follow-up within a day keeps you top of mind and shows you're serious. It's the easiest thing on this list and most people skip it.

Job Seeker: I'll remember that. This has made me feel far more in control of the whole process.

[Scene: The Coach nods and closes the notepad, satisfied.]

Coach: Good. Preparation is the antidote to nerves. You can't control the outcome, but you can control how ready you are — and that's exactly what we built today.
