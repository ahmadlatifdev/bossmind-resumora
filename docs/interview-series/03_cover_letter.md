# Episode 3 — Write a Cover Letter That Works

**Topic:** Writing a compelling, specific cover letter that complements the resume.
**Runtime:** ~8–10 minutes.

[Scene: The Career Coach and Job Seeker sit in the office, a blank document open on the screen in front of them.]

Coach: Today we're writing a cover letter. Most people treat it as a formality, but a strong one can be the reason you get a closer look.

Job Seeker: Honestly, I've never been sure what a cover letter is even for. Doesn't the resume already say everything?

[Scene: Close up on the Coach shaking their head and leaning forward.]

Coach: Not quite. The resume is a list of facts. The cover letter is your chance to connect those facts to this specific job and to show why you're a good fit. It's a story, not a summary.

Job Seeker: How long should it be? I've heard one page is a hard rule.

[Scene: Screen share of a clean, three-paragraph letter.]

Coach: Keep it to three or four short paragraphs, around half a page. Recruiters read dozens a day, so brevity is a gift. If it fills a whole page, it's too long.

Job Seeker: What should the first paragraph do?

[Scene: The cursor highlights the opening paragraph of the letter.]

Coach: The first line should name the exact role and why you're writing. Then, in one or two sentences, state your most relevant qualification. You want them to know within seconds why this letter exists.

Job Seeker: Can you show me an example opening?

[Scene: The Coach types into the blank document: "I'm applying for the Marketing Coordinator role because my two years building email campaigns map directly to the work your team does each week."]

Coach: Something like that. Specific, direct, and it connects you to them immediately. Compare that to "I am writing to express my interest," which says nothing.

Job Seeker: The difference is night and day. What goes in the middle paragraph?

[Scene: The Coach scrolls down to the body of the letter.]

Coach: The middle is your proof. Pick one or two accomplishments that match the job description, and tell them briefly. Use a concrete number when you can, because it makes the claim feel real.

Job Seeker: Should I repeat the exact bullets from my resume?

[Scene: The Coach shakes their head while pointing between the resume and the letter.]

Coach: No. Don't copy your bullets. Expand on one of them with a little more context. The resume says what you did; the letter can say how you did it and why it matters here.

Job Seeker: What about the closing paragraph?

[Scene: The cursor highlights the final paragraph.]

Coach: The closing should do two things: express genuine interest in this company, and make a simple ask — an interview or a conversation. End with a polite, confident sign-off.

Job Seeker: Should I mention the company's name specifically, or is that optional?

[Scene: Close up on the Coach nodding enthusiastically.]

Coach: Always mention the company and the role by name. It's the single easiest way to show you didn't send a form letter. A generic letter is worse than no letter at all.

Job Seeker: That's a strong statement. Why is a generic letter worse?

[Scene: The Coach leans back, gesturing with an open hand.]

Coach: Because it signals low effort. A hiring manager reads it and thinks you didn't care enough to personalize. No letter, or a short honest note, is better than a copy-paste template.

Job Seeker: What if the job posting doesn't give me much to personalize with?

[Scene: The Job Seeker looks slightly worried, then the Coach smiles.]

Coach: Then personalize to what you can see — the company's products, their recent news, or the team's obvious focus. Even one specific observation makes the letter feel human.

Job Seeker: Should I address it to a specific person if I don't know the name?

[Scene: The Coach points to the greeting of the letter.]

Coach: If you can find the hiring manager's name, use it. If not, "Dear Hiring Team" is fine. Never use "To Whom It May Concern" — it sounds dated and cold.

Job Seeker: What tone should I use? Formal or conversational?

[Scene: Close up on the Coach tilting their head, considering.]

Coach: Professional but warm. Write the way you'd speak in a good interview — clear, confident, and respectful, but not stiff. Avoid jargon and overused phrases like "synergy."

Job Seeker: Should I mention salary or my current situation anywhere in the letter?

[Scene: The Coach shakes their head firmly.]

Coach: No. Keep salary and personal circumstances out of the letter. That's a conversation for later in the process, not an opening move.

Job Seeker: How do I avoid sounding desperate, even if I really want this job?

[Scene: The Coach leans in and speaks softly.]

Coach: Focus on what you can offer them, not what you need from them. Confidence comes from describing your value clearly. Let the evidence speak instead of begging for a chance.

Job Seeker: That makes a lot of sense. Can we write a draft for one of my target roles right now?

[Scene: The Coach pulls up a job posting and begins typing, the Job Seeker leaning in to watch.]

Coach: Absolutely. We'll use your email campaign experience as the anchor, name the role and company, and end with a simple ask for a conversation. You'll see how fast it comes together.

Job Seeker: This feels so much more doable than I expected. The letter isn't a separate chore — it's the same story told differently.

[Scene: The Coach finishes typing and turns the screen toward the Job Seeker.]

Coach: Exactly. The resume, the letter, and later the interview are all one story. Tell it consistently, and you give yourself a better chance to be remembered. That's all any of these tools can do — they don't guarantee anything, but they help you show up well.

[Scene: The Job Seeker studies the draft, then looks up with another question.]

Job Seeker: Should I mention something specific I've learned about the company, like a recent product launch?

[Scene: Close up on the Coach nodding enthusiastically.]

Coach: Yes, that's gold. One specific reference to their work shows you did real research. Just keep it genuine — mention something you actually understand, not a random headline you skimmed.

Job Seeker: What if I'm changing industries and my background doesn't obviously match?

[Scene: The Coach leans forward and gestures to bridge the two areas.]

Coach: Then your letter does double duty. Use it to connect your transferable skills to the new industry, and explain the "why" behind your pivot. A resume can't tell that story, but a letter can.

Job Seeker: Is it ever okay to skip a cover letter if the application says it's optional?

[Scene: The Coach tilts their head, then answers carefully.]

Coach: If it's optional, a strong one still helps you stand out, especially for roles you're excited about. If it's required and you skip it, you're filtered out immediately. When in doubt, write a short, specific one.

Job Seeker: How should I open the letter if I was referred by someone?

[Scene: The Coach points to the greeting line.]

Coach: Lead with the referral right away. "Alex Chen suggested I reach out about the analyst role." A personal connection is the strongest opener you can have, so don't bury it.

Job Seeker: And what's the best way to close — just "thank you for your time"?

[Scene: The Coach types a closing line and highlights it.]

Coach: End with a confident, specific ask: "I'd welcome the chance to discuss how my background can support your team." Then a simple sign-off. Gracious, but clear about what you want.

Job Seeker: This has been incredibly clarifying. I feel like I finally understand what a cover letter is actually for.

[Scene: The Coach finishes typing and turns the screen toward the Job Seeker, smiling.]

Coach: That's the goal. It's your chance to connect the dots for a busy reader — honestly and clearly. That won't guarantee you the job, but it gives your application a real voice.
